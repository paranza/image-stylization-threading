The art behind the threads

A string art portrait has no paint, no grey and no brush. It is one dark line, wound thousands of times between pegs. Every tone you see comes from how often that line crosses a spot. Artists have built pictures this way for centuries. This page links the algorithm to those ideas.

1. Value comes first

Value is how light or dark a tone is, apart from its hue. Drawing teachers say that if the values are right, the picture reads, even in the wrong colors. A black and white photo works for the same reason.

To make values easier to judge, artists use a value scale: a strip of steps from white to black. The American painter and teacher Denman Ross used a 9 step scale, and the Munsell color system measures value from 0 (black) to 10 (white). Painters often make a small value study with only 3 to 5 values before the real work, to check that the big shapes of light and dark hold up.

In the tool: the Value steps control turns the target into a value study, and the Value study view shows it. The solver only ever looks at value per thread, never at hue directly.

2. Hatching: tone from lines

Engravers and pen draughtsmen cannot mix grey. They build tone from lines instead. Hatching is a set of close parallel lines; cross-hatching lays a second set across the first. Closer lines, or more layers, read as darker. Albrecht Dürer's engravings, such as Melencolia I (1514), show how far this can go: skin, metal, stone and sky, all made from lines of one black ink.

String art is hatching with one strict rule: each stroke is a straight line from one peg on the frame to another. You cannot stop a line halfway. Tone in a small area must come from many long lines that happen to cross there. That is why string portraits have their typical web of light rays and a soft, glowing look.

In the tool: each segment is one hatching stroke. Opacity is the pressure of the pencil (how much one stroke darkens), thickness is the nib, and pegs set how finely the strokes can be placed.

3. Layers of thread, layers of glaze

A thread is not fully opaque: light passes between and through the fibers. Two crossing threads let through less light than one, but not zero. Oil painters know this from glazing: thin transparent layers of paint over a dry layer. Each glaze lets through a share of the light, so the shares multiply.

The solver models thread the same way. Each pixel remembers how much light it still shows. A pass of thread with opacity 30% keeps 70% of it, a second pass keeps 70% of that, and so on. Because the tone of the drawing is known at every moment, the solver can ask a precise question for each candidate line: how much closer to the picture would the drawing get if I laid one more pass here?

4. Chiaroscuro and value key

Chiaroscuro (Italian for "light-dark") is the use of strong value contrast to model form and set mood. Leonardo used soft transitions (sfumato); Caravaggio pushed it to tenebrism, figures lit as if by a single lamp against black. Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring, the sample picture in the tool, sets a lit face and a bright pearl against a near black ground.

The key of a picture is where most of its values live. A high key picture is mostly light values; a low key picture is mostly dark ones.

In the tool: Value key shifts the picture toward the light or dark end, and Chiaroscuro applies an S-curve that deepens darks and brightens lights. String art loves contrast: thread can only add dark (or light, on a dark ground), so clear value masses come through best.

5. Optical mixing: color in the eye

Thin lines of different colors, seen from a distance, blend into a new color in the eye. This is optical mixing. The chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul studied how neighbouring colors affect each other (1839), and Georges Seurat built his pointillist paintings from small dots of pure color. Printed photos still work this way: tiny halftone dots of cyan, magenta, yellow and black.

On a white ground, thread works like ink: it removes light. The three color mode then uses the subtractive primaries. A cyan thread absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue. On a black ground, thread reflects light onto the dark, so the tool switches to the additive primaries: red, green and blue.

In the tool: in Three colors mode each thread solves its own channel and the solver always moves the thread that can improve the picture most, so the colors stay woven together. Use Squint to see the colors mix.

6. Squinting

A classic studio habit: squint at your subject. Details and edges blur away and only the big value masses remain, which makes it easier to check that the darks and lights are in the right place. A string portrait is meant to be seen this way, from a few steps back.

In the tool: the Squint slider blurs the view, so you can judge the work as a viewer across the room would.

7. Where string art comes from

In the late 1800s, the teacher Mary Everest Boole had children stitch straight lines between holes on cards, and saw smooth curves appear from straight lines. This "curve stitching" became the string art of the 1960s and 70s, with nails and colored yarn on boards. In 2016 the Greek artist Petros Vrellis showed portraits made with a single black thread wound around a circular frame of pins, the path computed by a program. That work made the technique famous, and this tool follows the same idea.

8. How the solver draws

  1. The picture is shrunk to a working size and toned (key, chiaroscuro, value steps).
  2. The drawing starts as a blank sheet: white, or black for a dark ground.
  3. The thread sits on a peg. For the line to every other reachable peg, the solver adds up how much one more pass would bring each pixel closer to the picture. A pass over a spot that is already dark enough counts against the line.
  4. It lays one pass along the best line, moves to the new peg, and repeats until no line would help.

This is what an artist does when hatching: look for the area that is still too light, and put the next stroke there, without overworking what is already done. The solver just does it thousands of times a second.
